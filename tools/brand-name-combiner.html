Startup naming frameworks

Every great brand name falls into one of three buckets: invented, descriptive, or evocative. Invented names are built from scratch or blended from fragments. Kodak was a word George Eastman made up because he liked the snap of the letter K, and Instagram is a straight blend of "instant" and "telegram". They cost more to explain at launch, but they are the easiest names to own: nobody else has them, matching domains are usually unclaimed, and under trademark law invented ("fanciful") words are the strongest category of mark you can get.

Descriptive names say what you do. Microsoft is literally "microcomputer" plus "software", zero confusion about the business, but every plain descriptive word in your industry is already taken, and you cannot stop competitors from using the same ordinary words. Evocative names sit in the middle: Amazon suggests scale without describing books at all, and Google riffs on "googol" (a 1 followed by a hundred zeros) to hint at infinite information. Evocative names give you room to grow far beyond your first product.

For a new startup, the sweet spot is usually an invented or evocative blend: distinctive enough to own outright, suggestive enough to remember after one hearing. That is exactly what this tool produces. Feed it founder names, product words, or the feeling you are going for, and it returns blends like Novake that live in that sweet spot. Naming by category

Different industries reward different sounds, and the category picker above is not decoration. It steers the name twists toward the phonetics your customers already trust. Tech buyers respond to short, clipped endings (-ly, -io, -hub) and hard consonants that sound fast and precise. Food brands win with warm, mouth-friendly sounds (-ery, -bite, -oven) you can almost taste. Fashion leans on soft, European-flavored endings (-elle, -ista, -chic) that signal taste before a single product photo loads.

The pattern holds in every aisle. Finance names need weight and solidity (-pay, -mint, -vault) because nobody hands money to something that sounds frivolous. Fitness brands need motion (-fit, -flex, -pulse). Pet brands need cuddliness (-paws, -buddy, -tails). Match the sound to the shelf you want to sit on and half your positioning is done before the logo exists. Mismatched sounds create friction you will pay for in every ad impression, a bank that sounds like a cupcake shop has an uphill climb no marketing budget fixes.

The domain & trademark checklist

Falling in love with a name you cannot own is the most expensive mistake in naming. Run this checklist on every finalist before you print a single business card:

1. Exact-match domain

Check whether the .com is available, or at least a clean alternative like .co or .io. If the .com belongs to a direct competitor, walk away. If it is parked, budget for the purchase before you commit to the name.

2. Social handles

Search the name on Instagram, TikTok, X, and YouTube. Consistent handles across platforms are worth more than a clever name with fragmented ones. Use our username generator to lock down matching handles for your new brand.

3. The search test

Search the exact name in quotes. If page one is dominated by an existing company, a product, or something unpleasant, pick another finalist. You will fight that association for years.

4. Trademark screen

Check your country's trademark database for identical or confusingly similar marks in your industry. This is a first screen, not a clearance, consult a trademark professional before filing anything or investing heavily in the brand.

Run the full checklist on your top three names, not just your favorite. Founders who skip it usually discover the conflict six months in, when rebranding costs real money and real customers.

Tests every brand name must pass

A name that looks clever on screen can die the first time a customer says it out loud. Put every finalist through these five tests. A name that fails two is not your name:

The radio test

Call a friend, say the name once, and ask them to spell it. If they cannot, every podcast mention, referral, and word-of-mouth moment leaks customers to whoever they did hear.

The bar test

Shout it across a noisy room. Names with mushy middles or near-identical soundalikes collapse in real-world noise; crisp consonants survive.

The ten-times test

Say it ten times fast. If it starts feeling awkward in your own mouth, your customers will feel it too, and they will shorten it into something you did not choose.

The plain-text test

Type it in black Arial with no logo, no styling. If it still looks like a brand, it is one. If it needs design tricks to feel real, the name is doing too little work.

The growth test

Imagine a second product line under the same name. "Novake" stretches from bakery to café; a name nailed to one product traps you the moment you expand.

A name that fails one test can sometimes be rescued, tweak the blend using the techniques in our guide to combining two names and run it through again. A name that fails two is telling you to move on to the next finalist.

Frequently asked questions

What is a brand name combiner?
A brand name combiner merges two words or founder names into a single catchy business name. It is a fast way to brainstorm startup names, product names and shop names that feel original.
How do I pick a good business name?
Aim for short, easy to spell and easy to say. Generate a long list of blends, then shortlist the ones that hint at what you do and sound confident when spoken aloud.
Should I check domain availability?
Yes, before you fall in love with a name, check whether the matching domain and social handles are available. A great name you cannot own online will only cause headaches later.
What makes a blended brand name work?
The best blends keep the strongest sound of each word, avoid awkward consonant pileups, and stay under three syllables. If strangers can spell it after hearing it once, you have a winner.
Can I trademark a blended name?
Invented words are often easier to protect than descriptive ones, but trademark rules vary by country. Treat the combiner as a brainstorming tool and consult a trademark professional before filing anything.
